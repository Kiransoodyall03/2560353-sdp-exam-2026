1 Service overview
The Radio Telescope Array (RTA) is a field of 100 radio dishes grouped into sub-arrays. A sub-array
scans a target in the sky, and the samples its dishes return form an image of the target. It is the highest-
rate service at the ARL. Topic prefix: arl/rta.

1.1 General state and routines
• Dishes in service: at start-up every dish is in main. About 80 to 90 dishes are in service; the rest are
  disabled for maintenance (enabled = false).
• Normal reporting: every dish that is not scanning reports its condition every 120 s on the normal
  stream.
• Scanning: while a sub-array scans, each of its dishes reports once a second on the scan stream
  instead. A scan divides the target’s field of view into a 128 × 128 grid and samples every grid cell 3
  times with different dishes, sharing the work between the sub-array’s dishes; with 90 dishes a scan
  takes about 9 minutes. The pointing offsets and signal strength of the samples are what build the
  image. A dish that drops out of a scan leaves its remaining samples untaken.
• Tracking: during a scan the dishes follow the target across the sky, so their azimuth and elevation
  change continuously.
• Targets: a target is either an id from the ARL API target catalogue or a custom sky position {ra_rad,
  dec_rad, fov_rad}. A scan needs its target at least 15° above the horizon to start, and fails if the
  target sets below 5°.
• Observation frequency: each sub-array observes at a frequency (default 1.42 GHz). Targets look
  different in different bands.
• Autonomous schedule (live deployment): the RTA plans its own scans every 45 to 90 minutes on the
  main sub-array, announced 5 minutes ahead through next_scan and scan_state = scheduled. About
  one scan in four also splits off 20 to 40 dishes into a temporary sub-array auto-2 to scan a second
  target at the same time; those dishes return to main afterwards. The schedule never overrides a sub-
  array a researcher has commanded in the last 2 hours. Scheduled scans have "origin": "schedule"
  in their scan events.
• Wind stow: when the gust at the array exceeds 15 m/s for 60 s, every dish stows (points straight up),
  running scans pause, and wind_stow becomes true. It releases once the gust has stayed below 10 m/s
  for 10 minutes. Paused scans then wait to be resumed.
• Dish mechanics: azimuth 0 to 2π (from north, wrapping), elevation 0.0873 to 1.5708 rad (5° to 90°).
  Azimuth slews at 2°/s, elevation at 1°/s.
• Load: under 1 message/s when idle; about 90 messages/s while main scans with 90 dishes.

Telemetry and event payloads carry an envelope: v (schema version, integer, currently 1), ts (simulated
time, Unix milliseconds), seq (per-topic sequence number, restarts with the service; gaps reveal lost
messages) and entity (entity id). The measurement fields are added next to the envelope. A
measurement that cannot be taken is null. State payloads are {"v", "ts", "entity", "item",
"value"}.

Commands are JSON {"command_id": <string>, "researcher": <researcher code>, "value":
<value>}. The researcher must be verified by the ARL API for the service. Acknowledgements carry
command_id, target, control, researcher, state (accepted, rejected, in_progress, completed,
failed), progress (0 to 1 for long-running controls), reason and detail. Rejection and failure reasons:
controls_disabled, malformed, unknown_control, unknown_target, unknown_researcher, researcher_
defunct, researcher_not_permitted, api_unavailable, invalid_value, precondition_failed, busy
(the target is already running a long command), fault, timeout, interrupted.
Alerts are events on arl/rta/events/alert with alert_id, type (the alert key below), severity (info,
warning, critical), state (raised or cleared), value, limit, unit, message and metadata. An alert
that escalates is raised again with the higher severity under the same alert_id. Limits come from the
ARL API (/v1/alert-limits/ + rta).

3 Scan images
The radio telescope produces a 2D image from the scan data. A scan divides the target’s field of view
into a square grid of 𝑁 × 𝑁 pixels, and every sample on the scan stream measures the brightness of one
pixel.
The grid comes from the scan’s started event (arl/rta/events/scan) and the samples from the dishes’
scan streams (matched by scan_id):

3.1 Sample to pixel
Each sample is taken at the centre of one pixel. The pixel (𝑥, 𝑦) of a sample, with 𝑥 the column (0 at the
left) and 𝑦 the row (0 at the top), is
                                                     Δ𝛼 1         1 Δ𝛿
                                   (𝑥, 𝑦) = (⌊𝑁 (       + )⌋, ⌊𝑁 ( −   )⌋)
                                                      𝜃  2        2  𝜃

so 𝑥 grows with the right ascension offset and 𝑦 grows downwards as the declination offset decreases
(north is at the top of the image). Equivalently, the offsets of the centre of pixel (𝑥, 𝑦) are

Offsets range over [− 2𝜃 , 2𝜃 ]; for an even 𝑁 (such as 128) the target centre falls between the four middle
pixels.

3.2 Pixel value
A pixel’s brightness is built from the samples that land on it:
                                                    1 𝑛
                                        𝐼(𝑥, 𝑦) =     ∑𝑠 ,          𝑛≤𝑘
                                                    𝑛 𝑖=1 𝑖

the mean signal_strength of the 𝑛 samples of that pixel. Samples whose offsets or signal are null (the
dish was still acquiring the target, or the scan was paused) carry no pixel and are skipped. A pixel with
no samples (for example because its dish dropped out of the scan) is a hole in the image.
To display the image, scale 𝐼 to a colour range, e.g. linearly from the minimum to the maximum pixel
value of the scan. Brighter pixels are stronger radio sources. Different bands (band_hz) of the same
target show different structure.

  Note: the scan data is noisy. Real telescope data is never clean, and neither is the ARL’s. Every
  sample contains random noise, dishes differ slightly in sensitivity, and faulty dishes can produce
  outliers far brighter than the rest of the image, or no data at all (see fault_code and snr_db). An
  image built directly from the raw samples may therefore look grainy, patchy or almost completely
  dark.
  Do not assume your first image is correct. Expect to process and clean up the samples before (or
  while) building the image, and check your result, for example against published images of the
  catalogue targets.

A Reference images
These are the correct images of the catalogue targets that have one, as an RTA scan of the target (at its
catalogue position and fov_rad) should reproduce them, without noise or faults. Use them to check your
reconstruction.
• Each image covers the target’s field of view, oriented as in Scan images: pixel row 0 (top) is north
  (positive declination offset), and column 0 (left) is the most negative right ascension offset.
• Each target has three bands. A scan uses the band whose frequency range contains the sub-array’s
  observation frequency, or else the band with the nearest centre: L (1 to 2 GHz, includes the default
  1.42 GHz), C (4 to 8 GHz) and K (18 to 26 GHz).
• Brightness is shown from black (0) to white (1). Your image will have a different absolute scale (and an
  offset from the sky background), so compare shapes and relative brightness, not values.
• Fornax A (tgt-for) and PKS 1934-638 (tgt-pks) have no reference image.
